It's September nineteen twenty-nine. You have one thousand dollars. Three doors: the stock market, a bank, or a shoebox under your bed. By nineteen thirty-three, one door is a disaster, one is a gamble, and one is a surprise. Let's play.
I'm Leo, and tonight I'm your host. I'm a cartoon, so nobody in this studio loses real money. The people of nineteen twenty-nine did. Their question was simple: where was money safe in nineteen twenty-nine? By the end, you'll know which door won, why it won, and why the same math runs the other way today.
Here's the idea this game will test: a safe place for money isn't really a place. It depends on what prices are doing. Now, how big is this thousand dollars? In nineteen twenty-nine, an average full-time worker earned about one thousand four hundred thirty dollars a year. So this is roughly eight months of pay. You don't want to lose it.
Here are the rules. You can't switch doors. You can't see the future. Each door gets a player. Stock Leo takes the blue door. Bank Leo takes the green door. And Box Leo takes the brown one, holding an actual shoebox. The studio audience laughs at Box Leo. Remember that laugh. Door one goes first, and in nineteen twenty-nine, it looks like the smartest choice in the room.
To see why, look at the last eight years. In August nineteen twenty-one, the Dow Jones Industrial Average stood at sixty-three. By September nineteen twenty-nine, it was at three hundred eighty-one. That's six times higher. Ordinary people, not just bankers, were putting their savings in. Many didn't even pay the full price. They put down part of it, sometimes as little as ten percent, and borrowed the rest. Stock Leo walks through the blue door on September third. That day, the Dow closes at three hundred eighty-one point one seven. It's the top. Nobody knows it yet.
For a few weeks, prices swing up and down, with sudden drops and quick recoveries. Then October arrives. On Monday, October twenty-eighth, the Dow falls nearly thirteen percent in a single day. On Tuesday, it falls almost twelve percent more. People will call them Black Monday and Black Tuesday. The borrowed money makes it worse. When prices drop, lenders want their loans covered, so people sell, and prices drop again. By mid-November, the Dow has lost almost half its value. Stock Leo's one thousand dollars is now about five hundred.
Half gone in about ten weeks. That hurts. But Stock Leo stays calm. He thinks this is a dip, and dips come back. Over at the green door, Bank Leo is even calmer. His money sits in a bank, with a vault and a marble floor. That's the safe door. Right?
Here's the part that surprises people today. In nineteen twenty-nine, a bank account in America had no federal deposit insurance. If your bank closed, nobody promised you'd get your money back. And there were a lot of banks. When the trouble began, over eight thousand banks belonged to the Federal Reserve System, and nearly sixteen thousand did not. Many were small and local. For a year, Bank Leo is fine. Then, in late nineteen thirty, banks start to fail in waves. Within a few weeks, hundreds close their doors.
On December eleventh, nineteen thirty, the fourth-largest bank in New York City, the Bank of United States, stops operating. Picture the street outside. A long line of people, all asking for their money at the same time. Here's the problem. A bank doesn't keep your thousand dollars in a box with your name on it. It lends most of it out. So when everyone comes at once, even a healthy bank can run out of cash. And when one bank falls, people at the next bank get nervous, and the line moves there.
Now the score for door two. It isn't one number. It's two. From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-three, about nine thousand banks suspended operations. That's more than one bank in three. Some reopened later. Thousands never did. If your bank was one of them, the FDIC's own numbers show depositors lost about twenty cents of every dollar, on average. For Bank Leo, that's around two hundred dollars gone, and he might wait a long time for the rest. If your bank survived, you kept everything. That's why door two is the gamble. It all depends on which bank you picked, and nobody could see inside.
Quick pause. If you like history played as a game, subscribing helps Leo build the next one. Now, back to the studio. One door is still closed. Box Leo. The one everybody laughed at. He hasn't touched his money once. And something strange is happening to it.
Box Leo's thousand dollars never changes. Same stack of bills, every morning. But the world around the box does change. Prices are falling. Not just a few prices. The average price of what families buy. The official price index shows it. From September nineteen twenty-nine to July nineteen thirty-two, consumer prices fell by about twenty-one percent. So the same thousand dollars now buys what about one thousand two hundred seventy dollars bought in nineteen twenty-nine. Box Leo did nothing, and his money got stronger.
Why were prices falling? Fewer jobs meant less spending. And money itself was shrinking. Federal Reserve History says the money supply fell by nearly thirty percent from the fall of nineteen thirty to the winter of nineteen thirty-three. Less money for the same goods means lower prices. Here's the strange part. While banks were failing, people across the country did exactly what Box Leo did. They pulled cash out and kept it at home. One shoebox looks safe. A whole country of shoeboxes made the banks weaker.
So Box Leo is the champion? Not so fast. A shoebox has its own traps. It earns nothing. It can burn. It can be stolen. And if you lose your job, you spend it, bill by bill, until the box is empty. Box Leo still has to survive the hardest moment of the whole game. Let's jump to the bottom.
July eighth, nineteen thirty-two. The Dow closes at forty-one point two two. From the top, that's a fall of about eighty-nine percent. Stock Leo's thousand dollars is now about one hundred and eight dollars. That's price only, before dividends. Even with falling prices, it buys only what about one hundred thirty-eight dollars bought in nineteen twenty-nine. Dividends did help, but look at the gap. So how long until he's back to one thousand? There are two answers, and they're far apart. The Dow's price didn't get back to its nineteen twenty-nine peak until November nineteen fifty-four. Counting dividends and falling prices, one well-known analysis puts the break-even point much earlier, in late nineteen thirty-six. Still, that's seven years of waiting.
Here's the scoreboard in July nineteen thirty-two. Stock Leo: about one hundred and eight dollars, on price alone. Bank Leo: a question mark. Maybe all of it, buying just as much as Box Leo's. Maybe around eight hundred, maybe still waiting. Box Leo: one thousand dollars that buy like twelve hundred seventy. Every result on this board belongs to that decade. A decade when prices were falling. That's what made a pile of cash look so clever.
But the game isn't over. It ends in March nineteen thirty-three. And March is when every door gets locked.
Winter, nineteen thirty-three. Panic jumps from state to state. Visitors who come to Washington for the new president's first day find notes in their hotel rooms: checks from out-of-town banks won't be accepted. By March fourth, every state has declared a bank holiday. Two days later, the new president, Franklin Roosevelt, closes every bank in the nation. Good banks and bad banks, all shut. Bank Leo stands at a locked door. For a few days, cash at home is suddenly very useful, and Box Leo is the only player holding any. When the holiday ends, about four thousand banks never reopen. Then, in June nineteen thirty-three, a new law creates federal deposit insurance. It starts on January first, nineteen thirty-four, and covers up to two thousand five hundred dollars per depositor.
In nineteen thirty-four, only nine insured banks suspended. The gamble door finally gets a lock that works. So Box Leo wins nineteen twenty-nine: the same gain in buying power, without the gamble. Now let's do something unfair. Let's take his winning door and move it to today.
Same shoebox. Same thousand dollars. Start in September twenty twenty-five. Twelve months later, [U.S.](/jˌuˈɛs/) consumer prices are three point seven percent higher. That's the official number for the year to September twenty twenty-six. So the thousand dollars now buys what about nine hundred sixty-four dollars bought a year earlier. Nothing was stolen. The bills are all there. They just buy less.
Now put the two periods side by side. From September nineteen twenty-nine to March nineteen thirty-three, prices fell about twenty-seven percent. From September twenty twenty-two to September twenty twenty-six, prices rose about thirteen and a half percent. Same box. Opposite result. In the early nineteen thirties, cash gained power because prices were falling. In twenty twenty-six, prices are rising, so the same math runs the other way. That's arithmetic, not a plan.
The bank door changed too. That lock from nineteen thirty-four is still there. Today, the FDIC says deposits are insured to at least two hundred fifty thousand dollars at each insured bank.
So, which door was safe? In nineteen twenty-nine, the door that looked smart hurt the most. The door everyone laughed at came out on top. Not because a shoebox is clever, but because of one thing outside all three doors: what prices were doing in that decade. That's the idea we started with. A safe place for money isn't really a place. It depends on what prices are doing, and on the rules around each door.
Falling prices made a shoebox the winner. But history also has a time when prices rose so fast that people's savings melted in a single year. That's a game for another night. I'm Leo. Thanks for playing.
